Yes. The distinction is quite useful in **problem solving**, especially when Prof. Saya talks about how we approach an unfamiliar problem.

### 1. Memory-based reasoning

Here, you recognise the problem as something you have **seen before**, and retrieve the solution or procedure from memory.

The mental process is roughly:

> **"I have seen this pattern → I remember the method → apply it."**

For example, suppose you are asked:

$$
\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx
$$

You remember:

> "This looks like the standard substitution \(u=x^2\)."

So you immediately apply the remembered procedure:

$$
u=x^2,\quad du=2x\,dx
$$

and obtain

$$
\sin(x^2)+C.
$$

You didn't necessarily reconstruct *why* this substitution is appropriate. You recognised the pattern.

---

### 2. Model-based reasoning

Here, instead of searching memory for a matching problem, you construct or use a **mental model of the situation** and reason from that model.

Suppose you are told:

> A car is travelling at 60 km/h. How far will it travel in 30 minutes?

You don't need to remember a particular problem.

You construct the model:

$$
\text{distance}=\text{speed}\times\text{time}
$$

Convert 30 minutes to \(0.5\) hour:

$$
d=60\times0.5=30\text{ km}.
$$

The important thing is that you understood the **relationship between the quantities**.

---

### The real difference

Think of it this way:

| Memory-based                           | Model-based                                            |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| "Have I seen this before?"             | "What is happening here?"                              |
| Retrieve a known solution              | Construct/use a representation                         |
| Pattern → solution                     | Model → reasoning → solution                           |
| Works very well for familiar problems  | Works better when the problem is unfamiliar            |
| Can fail when the problem is disguised | Can handle variations of the same underlying situation |

### A very simple analogy

Suppose you know how to get from **your house to the railway station**.

**Memory-based:**

> "Turn left here, right there, then take the second left."

If one road is closed, you may be stuck.

**Model-based:**

> "The station is north of my house, and the main road goes north. I need to get onto a road that eventually connects to the station."

Now, if one road is closed, you can **work out another route**.

That is the power of a model.

---

### And this connects beautifully to learning

A student who has memorised 100 solved problems may appear very good at problem solving.

But give him:

> **Problem 101, which looks different but is governed by the same underlying principle**

and he may struggle.

A student who has built the underlying model may say:

> **"This looks different, but the structure is the same."**

That is often the transition from **solving problems** to **understanding problems**.

And I suspect this is exactly why Prof. Saya distinguishes the two: **memory tells you what worked before; a model lets you reason about what should work now.**
